MATERIAL ENTRIES

Wieland Zenostar zirconia (full-ceramic, monolithic or high-translucency layered): Wieland Zenostar is a premium multilayer zirconia from Wieland Dental. It produces a natural colour gradient from a more opaque cervical zone to a translucent incisal zone, making it well suited to anterior and premolar restorations where both strength and aesthetics are required. The flexural strength ranges from approximately 700 to 900 MPa depending on the grade and zone of the multilayer block. Wieland Zenostar is available in two configurations: monolithic (full-contour, no porcelain overlay), and high-translucency layered (zirconia coping with hand-applied porcelain). Monolithic Wieland Zenostar is recommended for posterior restorations and cases where fracture resistance is the priority. High-translucency layered Wieland Zenostar is recommended for anterior restorations where maximum aesthetics are required. It is not recommended for patients with severe bruxism or for long-span posterior bridges under heavy occlusal load — consider monolithic zirconia or PFM in those cases. It is fully metal-free, which eliminates the risk of dark metal margins appearing at the gumline over time.

IPS e.max lithium disilicate (full-ceramic, pressed or CAD-milled): IPS e.max is a lithium disilicate glass-ceramic produced by Ivoclar Vivadent. It offers the highest level of translucency and natural light transmission of any material we work with, making it the preferred choice for upper anterior restorations where mimicking natural tooth appearance is the clinical priority. Its flexural strength is approximately 360 to 400 MPa, which is lower than zirconia — it is therefore not suitable for posterior full-arch restorations, patients with bruxism, or bridges spanning more than three units. IPS e.max crowns can be bonded adhesively, which permits more conservative tooth preparation than PFM. We offer IPS e.max in both pressed and CAD-milled versions. Pressed IPS e.max generally delivers a slightly more lifelike aesthetic result for complex anterior crowns and veneers. CAD-milled IPS e.max is standard for single crowns and shorter spans.

Monolithic zirconia (high-strength, full-contour): Monolithic zirconia is a full-contour restoration milled from a single zirconia block with no separate porcelain layer. Its flexural strength ranges from 1,000 to 1,200 MPa, making it the strongest material we offer and the recommended choice for posterior restorations in patients with bruxism, heavy bite forces, or a history of chipping porcelain restorations. Because there is no porcelain overlay, the risk of chipping is effectively eliminated. Aesthetics are acceptable for posterior teeth but are not comparable to layered zirconia or IPS e.max for anterior use — the material has lower translucency and a more opaque appearance. It is available in standard white and multi-layered gradient shades for improved colour blending in visible posterior positions.

PFM crown (porcelain-fused-to-metal — Vita VM9 porcelain over cobalt-chromium alloy): PFM crowns consist of a cast metal substructure with Vita VM9 porcelain fired on top. They have a long clinical track record and remain a reliable, cost-effective choice for posterior restorations where strength is prioritised over maximum aesthetics. The metal substructure provides excellent marginal fit and long-term durability. The primary aesthetic limitation is the potential for a dark metal margin line to become visible at the gumline if gum recession occurs over time. PFM is suitable for patients with heavy bite forces, long-span bridges, and cases where budget does not support full-ceramic alternatives. Our standard PFM uses Vita VM9 porcelain over a cobalt-chromium alloy base. Precious metal alloy (gold-based) substructures are available on request at additional cost.

PMMA provisional crown (polymethyl methacrylate, temporary): PMMA is used for provisional or temporary restorations while a permanent restoration is being fabricated or during an implant osseointegration period. It is not intended as a long-term solution. PMMA provisionals are lightweight, tooth-coloured, and easy to adjust chairside. They have lower fracture resistance than ceramic or zirconia and should not be used in high-load posterior positions for extended periods. Standard lead time for a PMMA provisional is 2 to 3 business days. We recommend PMMA for implant cases requiring an extended osseointegration period of 3 months or more, and for cases where the final shade or form needs to be evaluated in the patient's mouth before the permanent restoration is confirmed.

Cobalt-chromium removable partial denture framework (cast CoCr with acrylic base and prosthetic teeth): A cobalt-chromium (CoCr) removable partial denture consists of a precision-cast metal framework with rests, clasps, and connectors, to which an acrylic denture base and prosthetic teeth are attached. CoCr partials offer excellent rigidity, long-term durability, and precise clasp retention, and are the standard of care for removable partial dentures where good posterior support and cross-arch stability are required. The metal framework distributes occlusal forces effectively across both tooth and tissue support. The visible metal clasps are a cosmetic limitation for some patients — in cases where clasp aesthetics are a primary concern, a Valplast flexible partial denture is an alternative. CoCr partials are not suitable for patients with a confirmed cobalt or chromium allergy.

Valplast flexible partial denture (thermoplastic nylon resin): Valplast is a metal-free thermoplastic material used for removable partial dentures. Its clasps are fabricated from the same gum-coloured nylon material as the denture base, making them nearly invisible when in place — this is its primary clinical advantage over conventional metal-clasp partial dentures. Valplast is recommended for patients who cannot receive a fixed restoration and who have an aesthetic concern about visible metal clasps. It is not suitable as a long-term replacement for extensive posterior support or cases requiring rigid cross-arch stability. Valplast partials cannot be relined or repaired in the same way as acrylic partials, and additions are not possible after fabrication. It is best suited for bounded saddle cases with good remaining dentition.

Acrylic removable partial denture (without metal framework): An acrylic removable partial denture uses an acrylic resin base with wire clasps and prosthetic teeth, without a cast metal framework. It is the most cost-effective removable prosthetic option and is most appropriate for short-term or transitional use. Because the acrylic base has lower rigidity and durability than a cobalt-chromium framework, acrylic partials are not recommended as a long-term replacement for posterior support and are not the first-line clinical recommendation for most patients.

PEEK framework (polyether ether ketone, implant-supported): PEEK is a high-performance thermoplastic polymer used for implant-supported frameworks, full-arch implant bridges, and some removable prosthetic superstructures. It is completely metal-free, very lightweight, and has excellent biocompatibility and osseous integration characteristics. PEEK is used as an alternative to titanium or zirconia for implant frameworks in patients with confirmed metal sensitivities, and for full-arch implant restorations where weight reduction is a clinical priority. PEEK has a flexural strength of approximately 100 MPa and is not suitable for single-unit crowns or restorations under direct heavy occlusal loading — it is best used in framework and superstructure applications where forces are distributed across the implant system. Please contact us to discuss case suitability before ordering.

MATERIAL COMPARISONS

Zirconia vs. PFM (porcelain-fused-to-metal): Zirconia crowns are entirely metal-free, show no dark metal margin line at the gumline, offer better long-term biocompatibility, and produce a more natural aesthetic result under different lighting conditions. PFM crowns are more cost-effective, have a longer published clinical track record, and remain a reliable choice for posterior restorations under heavy bite forces. For anterior teeth or patients with metal sensitivity, zirconia is generally preferred. For posterior teeth in patients with bruxism, long-span bridges, or significant budget constraints, PFM remains a sound clinical choice.

IPS e.max vs. Wieland Zenostar zirconia (layered) for anterior restorations: IPS e.max produces a more lifelike, translucent result than layered zirconia for anterior restorations because its optical properties more closely mimic natural enamel. However, IPS e.max has lower fracture resistance (approximately 360 to 400 MPa versus 700 to 900 MPa for layered Wieland Zenostar zirconia) and is not suitable for high-load posterior positions or bruxism cases. For a single upper anterior crown where the highest aesthetic outcome is the priority and the patient has no parafunctional habits, IPS e.max is the preferred choice. For premolars, posterior teeth, or patients with parafunctional habits, Wieland Zenostar zirconia is the safer option.

Monolithic zirconia vs. layered zirconia: Monolithic zirconia is a single milled block with no porcelain overlay, giving it a flexural strength of 1,000 to 1,200 MPa and eliminating chipping risk entirely. Layered zirconia uses porcelain applied over a zirconia coping, producing a more natural and translucent appearance — but the porcelain layer carries a small risk of chipping under heavy load. For posterior teeth, especially in bruxism patients or cases with limited interocclusal space, monolithic zirconia is the preferred choice. For anterior teeth where maximum aesthetics are required, layered zirconia (or IPS e.max) is the better choice despite the marginally higher chipping risk.

Zirconia vs. IPS e.max for a single upper central incisor: For a single upper central incisor where the patient has a high aesthetic demand and a natural adjacent tooth to match, IPS e.max is generally the first choice due to its superior translucency and light-scattering properties. High-translucency layered Wieland Zenostar zirconia is an acceptable alternative if the patient has a history of porcelain chipping or if the dentist prefers a stronger material. Standard (non-high-translucency) monolithic zirconia is not recommended for upper central incisors in aesthetically demanding cases due to its more opaque appearance.

PFM vs. monolithic zirconia for a posterior molar in a bruxism patient: For a posterior molar in a patient with confirmed bruxism, monolithic zirconia is the preferred choice. It has higher strength than PFM and eliminates the risk of porcelain chipping entirely. PFM remains acceptable if the patient has significant budget constraints, but the Vita VM9 porcelain layer in a PFM crown can chip under repeated heavy occlusal loading in bruxism patients, requiring repair or replacement. Monolithic zirconia is the more durable long-term solution for this clinical scenario.

IPS e.max veneer (pressed) vs. other veneer options: Pressed IPS e.max is our recommended material for anterior veneers where maximum translucency and a natural aesthetic result are the priority. It is bonded adhesively, allowing for conservative preparation. It is not suitable for patients with bruxism or parafunctional habits. Zirconia veneers are an alternative for patients who require a stronger material, but they are less translucent and less frequently indicated for veneers. If the patient has a history of chipping or heavy anterior contact, a full crown in IPS e.max or high-translucency Wieland Zenostar zirconia may be more appropriate than a veneer.

Cobalt-chromium framework partial vs. Valplast flexible partial vs. acrylic partial denture: A cobalt-chromium (CoCr) framework partial is the recommended first-line clinical choice for most patients requiring a removable partial denture. It provides the best long-term rigidity, support, and durability, and its metal framework distributes forces effectively across tooth and tissue support. A Valplast flexible partial is the preferred option when the patient cannot tolerate visible metal clasps and does not require extensive posterior support — its gum-coloured nylon clasps are nearly invisible. An acrylic partial without a metal framework is the most cost-effective option and is best suited for transitional or short-term use only. Valplast and acrylic partials are not recommended as long-term replacements for posterior support in patients with multiple missing posterior teeth.

PMMA provisional vs. permanent ceramic restorations: PMMA provisionals are intended only for temporary use during the treatment planning or fabrication period. They provide adequate aesthetics and function for short-term wear but have significantly lower strength and durability than permanent ceramic or zirconia restorations. PMMA should not be used as a long-term restoration in high-load positions. Once the permanent restoration is confirmed, the PMMA provisional should be replaced promptly.
